Introduction: empire, objects, and a
changing field
Incantation bowls are ordinary ceramic vessels transformed into ritual objects by spiral, concentric,
radial, or otherwise arranged inscriptions and, often, drawings. Most belong to late antique
Mesopotamia, especially the Sasanian and immediately post-Sasanian centuries. The best-supported
central date range is approximately 500-700 CE, with the densest evidence in the sixth and seventh
centuries, rather than the very broad third-eighth-century range sometimes repeated in popular
accounts. A small group carries absolute dates in the sixth and early seventh centuries, and excavated
contexts allow some continuation into the early Islamic period. The principal languages and scripts
are Jewish Babylonian Aramaic, Mandaic, and Syriac; Middle Persian in Pahlavi script and invented
or pseudo-script bowls form related parts of the material field.
That chronology places the bowls inside one of the great political transformations of western Asia. The Sasanian
dynasty ruled from 224 to 651 CE. Mesopotamia was not a remote fringe: its irrigated plains, cities, tax base, and the
royal region around Ctesiphon made it a western heartland of the empire. The dynasty promoted an Iranian kingship
closely associated with Zoroastrian institutions, but the population of Mesopotamia was religiously and linguistically
plural. Jewish, Church of the East and other Christian, Mandaean, Manichaean, Zoroastrian, and locally rooted
communities shared cities, markets, administrative structures, languages, stories, and ritual specialists without
dissolving into a single culture. The bowls are products of this imperial society, not objects that merely happen to be
dated by Sasanian rulers.
The main bowl horizon coincides with the empire's final long century. Under Khusrau I (531-579), centralizing reforms
and renewed imperial power intensified the institutional world in which late antique Mesopotamians lived. Subsequent
revolts and the prolonged Roman-Sasanian war of 602-628 destabilized the empire; succession struggles followed,
Arab-Muslim armies took most of Iraq in the 630s and 640s, and the last Sasanian king died in 651. Bowl production did
not halt on a single political date. Objects in early Islamic levels show continuity across regime change, while shifts in
language, administration, settlement, and religious authority altered the environment in which the practice had
flourished. The bowls therefore preserve household-scale evidence from the last Sasanian generations and the
transition into early Islamic Mesopotamia. For the political frame, see the Metropolitan Museum's historical overview; for
the argument that Jewish life must be studied within, not merely against the background of, Sasanian imperial
structures, see Simcha Gross's Babylonian Jews and Sasanian Imperialism in Late Antiquity (Cambridge).
The corpus is exceptionally important because it is domestic, named, and materially situated. Bowl texts frequently
identify clients, family members, houses, adversaries, illnesses, reproductive dangers, demons, or other threats. They
therefore illuminate forms of everyday religious action only obliquely represented in legal, theological, and literary texts.
For late antique Babylonian Judaism in particular, the Jewish bowls are the only large surviving written corpus besides
the Babylonian Talmud, or Bavli, from approximately the same region and broad period. The Bavli is a vast, layered
rabbinic compilation of law, debate, interpretation, and narrative; it is not a transparent transcript of all Babylonian
Jewish life. Reading bowls beside it places prescriptive and elite literary voices in conversation with personalized ritual

The history of scholarship is itself part of the evidence. Thomas Ellis's 1853 decipherments made the bowls legible to
European scholarship, while James A. Montgomery's 1913 Nippur edition created the first durable corpus and
numbering system. Cyrus H. Gordon and other mid-century editors multiplied the available texts, sometimes faster than
photographs, archaeological documentation, or dialect knowledge could support. Joseph Naveh and Shaul Shaked
then showed how exact philology and formula comparison could correct inherited readings; Dan Levene, J. B. Segal,
Christa Müller-Kessler, Marco Moriggi, James Nathan Ford, Matthew Morgenstern, Siam Bhayro, and their
collaborators built the museum and language corpora on which comparison now depends.
At the same time, scholars changed the questions. Erica C. D. Hunter returned bowls to excavated houses and
depositional contexts. Michael G. Morony made Sasanian society central rather than decorative background. Gideon
Bohak and Yuval Harari1 placed the bowls within longer histories of Jewish magic, while Jason Sion Mokhtarian, Avigail
Manekin-Bamberger, Shai Secunda, and others recast the relationship with rabbinic culture as uneven participation in a
shared Babylonian world. Naama Vilozny treated images as operative, not ornamental; Ortal-Paz Saar and
Manekin-Bamberger separated client, beneficiary, textual voice, copyist, and author in gender analysis; Daniel James
Waller made biblical quotation and written performativity auditable; and recent digital, multispectral, and provenance
work has made the object, image, collection history, and data model as important as the edited text.
Across that history, three major interpretive shifts organize the present review:
 1.  From curiosities to corpora. Nineteenth-century decipherments and James A. Montgomery's 1913 Nippur edition
 established the objects as linguistic and religious evidence. Later museum catalogues and corpus editions made
comparative work possible.
2.  From “popular magic” to heterogeneous scribal practice. Earlier scholarship often opposed bowls to “normative”
 or rabbinic religion. Current research sees varied practitioners, clients, learned traditions, local formulae, and
degrees of contact with rabbinic, liturgical, legal, biblical, Mandaean, Christian, Iranian, and older Mesopotamian
materials. The corpus does not represent a single institution or social class.
3.  From text alone to object, household, network, and data. Archaeology, materiality, images, handwriting,
 prosopography, gender, household history, provenance ethics, multispectral imaging, and digital ontology now stand
beside philology. This has also weakened the assumption that script directly proves the religious identity of either
client or practitioner.
The most durable conclusion is that there was no single “bowl religion” and probably no uniform ritual. The texts share
technologies - binding, sealing, banning, divorcing, adjuring, invoking, narrating, quoting, drawing, and materially
depositing - but recombine them in local and sometimes highly individual ways. Their efficacy could lie in language,
divine or angelic authority, visual form, the permanence and placement of writing, the containment symbolism of the
vessel, a spoken performance, or several of these at once.

The main obstacle to a fully systematic review is not lack of scholarship but an unstable evidence base. Several
thousand bowls are reported or known, while only a fraction have reliable editions, photographs, secure find contexts,
or uncontested provenance. Counts change as objects are published, reidentified, or linked across catalogues. Daniel
James Waller counted more than 460 published JBA2 bowls by November 2022 in The Bible in the Bowls (open-access
book); the European Jewish Classical Magic project describes approximately 500 JBA magical objects, about 125
Mandaic bowls, and about 50 Syriac magical objects in its working corpora (EJCM3). Those figures are methodological
working counts, not a census of all extant bowls. A full-scope review must therefore keep three levels distinct: the
material corpus believed to exist, the published corpus that can be searched, and the smaller verified corpus for which
image, edition, and provenance can be evaluated together.
1. Review method, scope, and limitations
This is a systematic scoping review, not a statistical meta-analysis and not a claim to have read
every publication in full. Incantation-bowl scholarship is dispersed among monographs, museum
catalogues, articles, excavation reports, dissertations, Festschriften, older publications in several
languages, and unpublished collection records. Many primary texts have also been re-edited under
different sigla.
1.1 Questions used to organize the review
 •  What objects constitute the corpus, and how securely can they be dated and located?
 •  How has the publication and interpretation of the corpus changed since 1853?
 •  What are the principal editions for each linguistic or script group?
 •  What do bowls contribute to the history of religion, language, ritual, households, gender, law, medicine, and textuality?
 •  Where do scholars genuinely disagree?
 •  Which evidence is sufficiently secure for historical or creative adaptation, and which remains provisional?
 •  What research procedure will keep the review current and make object-level claims auditable?
 
1.2 Sources included
Priority was given to:
 •  primary editions and museum catalogues;
 •  archaeological reports and museum collection descriptions;
 •  peer-reviewed linguistic, historical, religious-studies, and material-culture research;
 •  dissertations that introduced substantial corpora or methods;
 •  official digital projects and institutional repositories;
 
2  Jewish Babylonian Aramaic, the principal Aramaic dialect and modern corpus label used for bowls written in Jewish square script.
3  European Jewish Classical Magic, a digital research project and structured corpus based at Ca' Foscari University of Venice.

2.1 Physical form and deposition
Most bowls are inexpensive, wheel-made ceramic household vessels, often already suitable as
everyday ware before inscription. Text normally proceeds in a spiral on the interior, but concentric,
radial, exterior, rim, and mixed arrangements occur. Drawn figures, circles, grids, magical signs, seals,
and pseudo-writing may be integral to the design.
Excavated examples were often deposited upside down beneath floors, at thresholds, in corners, or around the edges
of domestic spaces; pairs were sometimes placed rim-to-rim. These patterns support interpretations involving
protection, enclosure, immobilization, or control of harmful forces. They do not prove that every bowl was conceived as
a literal demon trap. Depositional practice, text, image, and intended result vary.
Erica C. D. Hunter's reassessment of the Nippur discoveries remains essential because it returns the bowls to houses
and strata rather than treating them only as manuscripts. The 1989 excavations recovered bowls in a stratified domestic
context, and proximity between bowls in different Aramaic scripts complicates simple maps of separate religious
neighborhoods. Hunter's article appears in the open Nippur VII volume (SAOC7 71 PDF). Penn's current work likewise
emphasizes that the institution holds a much larger and less selectively published Nippur assemblage than Montgomery
edited - five displayed objects and roughly 285 in storage at the time of the report (Penn Today).
2.2 Geography
The heartland is central and southern Mesopotamia, especially sites in present-day Iraq. Nippur is crucial because of
excavation, but the market corpus ranges across Iraq and western Iran, frequently without a reliable findspot. Khouabir,
Borsippa/Birs Nimrud, Kutha, and other named locations appear in early reports; many such attributions require
rechecking against excavation history.
The find distribution is therefore partly ancient and partly modern: excavation choices, colonial collecting, dealer
networks, wars, museum acquisition, legibility, and scholarly access all shape the map. A bowl said to be “from Nippur”
may mean excavated at Nippur, stylistically attributed to Nippur, acquired with other Nippur material, or merely assigned
a dealer provenance. These categories must remain separate.

2.3 Sasanian Mesopotamia as the bowls' historical setting
The Sasanian Empire began when Ardashir I defeated the last Arsacid ruler in 224 CE and ended with the death of
Yazdgard III in 651. Its political center moved across an imperial zone that joined the Iranian plateau to the
Mesopotamian lowlands, but the royal capitals near Ctesiphon placed the great cities and agricultural districts of Iraq
close to power. Imperial taxation, landholding, military movement, courts, legal traditions, roads, and markets shaped
the same households in which bowls were commissioned and deposited.
Zoroastrianism had a privileged relationship to Sasanian kingship, and Zoroastrian priests and fire temples appear in
contemporary Jewish and Christian texts. That privilege should not be simplified into a modern model of one uniformly
enforced state religion. Sasanian Mesopotamia contained long-established Jewish populations, several Christian
churches and monastic networks, Mandaeans, Manichaeans, Iranian religious communities, and adherents of other
local traditions. Aramaic dialects remained widespread, while Middle Persian supplied imperial, administrative, legal,
and onomastic vocabulary. Iranian personal names and supernatural beings in the bowls are therefore evidence of
ordinary imperial entanglement, not necessarily the religious conversion of a client or scribe.
The political sequence helps interpret, but does not mechanically date, the objects. The sixth century included the long
reign of Khusrau I (531-579), major administrative and fiscal change, urban and agricultural continuity, and recurrent
war with the Roman Empire. The final war of 602-628 reached an extraordinary scale, after which dynastic instability
and conquest transformed the regime. Muslim rule in Iraq was established during the 630s and 640s, before the
Sasanian dynasty ended in 651. Bowls in early Islamic contexts show that ritual and scribal habits crossed this
boundary. Consequently, "Sasanian bowl" is best used as a historical orientation, not as a claim that every object
predates 651 or expresses official Iranian religion.
This history also changes how the scholarship is framed. Morony's social histories asked what named clients, Iranian
names, work, kinship, and shared demonologies reveal about late Sasanian Iraq. More recently, Simcha Gross has
argued that Babylonian Jewish institutions and texts were formed within Sasanian political conditions rather than in a
self-contained Jewish sphere. Siam Bhayro's work on absolutely dated bowls and the 2025 field volume bring those
imperial and chronological questions into object-level study. The result is a history in which bowls, the Bavli, Syriac
literature, Mandaic sources, and Iranian evidence are separate archives of the same world, not isolated illustrations of
predetermined communities.
2.4 Dating
The safest working range is ca. 500-700 CE, with the densest evidence in the sixth and seventh centuries. Dated bowls
include specimens assigned to 545, 573/574, 580, 585, and 611 CE. Archaeological and palaeographic arguments may
extend individual pieces earlier, while finds in early Islamic levels show that the practice did not end everywhere at the
Sasanian collapse. The 2025 field volume explicitly frames the main Jewish corpus as late Sasanian to early Islamic
(Brown Judaic Studies).

The question "Where do bowl incantations come from?" needs to be divided into at least three
questions: where the bowl-as-ritual-object developed, where its reusable formulae and stories
came from, and where the individual composition for a named household was made. The
answers are not identical. A cheap ceramic vessel could be a specifically Mesopotamian ritual medium
even when the words written on it included biblical verses, legal formulae, translated Mandaic
material, Iranian names, or stories with a much longer history.

3.1 The origin of the bowl practice
No surviving source narrates the invention of the practice, and the apparent suddenness of the archaeological record
may be partly a problem of preservation. Gideon Bohak has asked whether comparable incantations were previously
spoken or written on perishable materials. Metal amulets, gems, lead rolls, parchment, ostraca, skulls, and manuals
show that late antique ritual writing already moved across media. What is distinctive is the large-scale Mesopotamian
choice of ordinary bowls, frequently written in spirals and installed in houses. Erica Hunter's archaeological work and
Waller's analysis of written performativity both make the local material setting indispensable: a bowl returned
specialized writing to the gestural world of a household vessel, then changed that vessel's use through inscription and
deposition.
Older Mesopotamian ritual traditions supplied a deep regional vocabulary of demons, witchcraft, binding, protection,
figurines, house ritual, and expert incantation. Similarity alone, however, does not demonstrate a continuous line from
cuneiform exorcistic series to a sixth-century Aramaic bowl. Languages, institutions, media, and textual forms changed
over many centuries. Claims of descent are strongest when a distinctive narrative sequence, technical expression, ritual
action, or demonological cluster can be traced through intermediate evidence; a general shared concern with hostile
spirits is only regional background.
3.2 Local composition and professional repertoires
Most of an individual bowl is best understood as a local composition assembled for a concrete problem. The scribe or
practitioner inserts a client's name, matronymic, spouse, children, house, property, adversary, symptoms, or feared
being into a working repertoire. Formula families, repeated hands, shared layouts, and recurrent errors show that some
practitioners copied exemplars or worked from memorized sequences. Yet variation is pervasive: passages are
expanded, shortened, recombined, translated, and redirected. "Local" therefore does not mean improvised from
nothing. It means that circulating materials acquired their operative form in a particular commission, workshop,
household, and social network.
Legal and administrative speech was one such local reservoir. Bowls bind, seal, ban, excommunicate, adjure, or
divorce harmful beings. Avigail Manekin-Bamberger's work demonstrates that divorce, oath, vow, and excommunication
language could be repurposed as supernatural acts. The evidentiary claim should remain proportional: a technical
resemblance can show familiarity with authoritative legal forms without proving that a rabbinic court wrote the bowl or
that a particular written legal handbook was copied.
3.3 Biblical and liturgical material
Biblical language is prominent but not universal, and even in Jewish bowls it is usually one component of a larger
incantation. Common materials include the Shema, the priestly blessing, psalms, Zechariah 3:2, Numbers 23:23, and
language from Exodus and other protective or triumphal passages. Daniel James Waller's The Bible in the Bowls
catalogues identifiable biblical quotations in published JBA bowls through November 2022. Dorota Molin and Daniel
Frim show why the evidence matters beyond ritual history: these are the only direct epigraphic witnesses to Biblical
Hebrew from late antique Babylonia, preserving spelling, pronunciation, reading, and memory practices before the
medieval vocalized codices.

Waller's catalogue also prevents an important exaggeration. Biblical content does not make a whole incantation
"biblical." A verse may supply one authoritative clause inside a text otherwise composed of adjurations, legal
metaphors, client names, magical names, drawings, and locally transmitted formulae. Quotations range from close
textual reproduction to abbreviation, conflation, Aramaic interference, and remembered wording. Some language may
have reached a bowl through prayer or another magical text rather than direct consultation of a biblical manuscript. The
safest vocabulary therefore distinguishes quotation, adaptation, allusion, and formula that also occurs in
scripture.
Liturgy is related but not reducible to Bible. The Shema and other prayer language could circulate through communal
recitation, household memory, written liturgical forms, or magical exemplars. Dan Levene's work on liturgical material
asks how recognizable prayer was repositioned inside a new ritual argument. Here again, verbal identity, sequence,
and distinctive variants matter more than a general resemblance.
3.4 Jewish literary, rabbinic, and magical traditions
The bowls share demons, rabbis, biblical interpretations, legal concepts, stories, and ritual assumptions with the Bavli
and with other Jewish magical evidence. Named figures such as Ḥanina ben Dosa and Joshua ben Peraḥiah can
authorize narratives of conquest over demons. Moses appears as lawgiver, master of divine names, and source of
esoteric authority. Naveh and Shaked's comparative editions, Bohak's historical synthesis, Harari's study of early
Jewish magic, and Mokhtarian's bowl-Talmud comparisons show a mobile repertoire that crossed amulets, manuals,
liturgy, narrative, and rabbinic learning.
This does not make the Bavli the master source for the bowls. The Babylonian Talmud is itself composite and was
transmitted and redacted over time. Exact dependence requires more than a shared demon or legal term: one should
look for unusual wording, the same narrative order, a distinctive error, or a cluster of correspondences that is unlikely to
arise from the common environment. In many cases, "shared Babylonian repertoire" is a more accurate conclusion than
"the bowl quotes the Talmud."
3.5 Mandaic, Syriac, Iranian, and wider late antique sources
Mandaic and Syriac bowls have their own formula families, linguistic histories, named powers, and ritual logics. They
must not be treated only as parallels to Jewish material. At the same time, close parallels demonstrate translation and
borrowing across scripts. Gross and Scarlassara's B9002 is especially valuable because multispectral imaging revealed
a Syriac-script bowl carrying a near-verbatim Mandaic incantation. Such a case permits a specific claim about textual
movement; the mere presence of a shared angelic name would not.
Iranian influence appears in personal names, administrative and legal vocabulary, cosmology, geography, demons, and
divine or astral beings. The bowls emerged in an Iranian empire, so these features are not exotic intrusions into an
otherwise sealed Aramaic world. Nor does an Iranian name prove that its bearer was Zoroastrian. The same caution
applies to Greek, Egyptian, or broader late antique magical names and signs: some belonged to widely circulating
professional repertoires whose users did not adopt the theology of every name they invoked.

3.6 A source-critical model
For each passage, the review therefore asks five increasingly demanding questions:
 1.  Identification: Is the wording recognizably connected to another text or only thematically similar?
 2.  Direction: Can the source be earlier than the bowl, or might both depend on a third repertoire?
 3.  Route: Was the material likely copied, remembered, translated, heard in ritual, or borrowed through another magical
 object?
4.  Transformation: What did the bowl add, omit, reorder, personalize, or materially reframe?
 5.  Function: What authority did the borrowed material claim in this specific incantation?
 This model makes "origin" a history of transmission and recomposition rather than a search for one parent tradition. The
most typical bowl is locally made and textually plural: its immediate composition belongs to a late antique
Mesopotamian practitioner, while its components may reach into scripture, prayer, law, story, Iranian imperial culture,
neighboring Aramaic traditions, older Mesopotamian ritual, and the wider technologies of ancient magic.
4. Chronological history of scholarship,
1853-2026
4.1 Discovery and decipherment, 1853-1912
Thomas Ellis's 1853 publication of bowls acquired by Austen Henry Layard is conventionally treated
as the beginning of JBA bowl scholarship. Nineteenth-century work by Michel Nicolas Schwab, Joseph
Halévy, Henry Hyvernat, Henri Pognon, and others tried to identify scripts, languages, divine names,
demons, and analogues in rabbinic or biblical literature. Mark Lidzbarski and James A. Montgomery's
near contemporaries improved Mandaic and Aramaic decipherment; J. A. Myhrman's 1909 edition
added material from the Hilprecht collection.
This period established three habits that still require caution:
 •  treating badly provenanced acquisitions as though their findspots were certain;
 •  “correcting” nonstandard language toward classical or literary Aramaic;
 •  using assumed communal identity to solve ambiguous readings.
 Its achievement was nonetheless fundamental: scholars recognized a coherent late antique ritual technology and
began developing the dialect knowledge needed to read it.

4.2 Montgomery and the Nippur foundation, 1913
James A. Montgomery's Aramaic Incantation Texts from Nippur (1913) published forty bowls and one inscribed skull,
with drawings or plates, glossary, grammatical discussion, religious parallels, and archaeological framing. It remains the
foundational point of reference; Cambridge's modern edition describes its forty bowl texts and forty-one illustrations
(book record).
Montgomery's strengths were breadth and comparativism. His weaknesses are those of an early edition: selective
publication, hand copies rather than modern imaging, readings made before the dialects were well understood, and
interpretive categories shaped by older “magic versus religion” models. Modern researchers must cite his numbering
because it anchors the field, but should not assume that his transcription is the last word.
CBS/B 2941, Montgomery no. 38, illustrates the continuing value and limitation of his numbering: it can be traced
across later scholarship as Yamauchi's Mandaic Text 23, but its individual readings still require comparison with the
object image and subsequent Mandaic work.
4.3 Expansion, classification, and compilation, 1930s-1970s
Cyrus H. Gordon published numerous bowls from collections in Europe, North America, and the Middle East from the
1930s onward. Other important contributions include J. Obermann, W. H. Rossell's 1953 dissertation, Edwin M.
Yamauchi, William S. McCullough, and Stephen A. Kaufman. The period greatly enlarged the known corpus but also
multiplied sigla and uneven hand copies.
Three books became landmarks:
 •  Edwin M. Yamauchi, Mandaic Incantation Texts (1967). This organized an early Mandaic corpus, grammar, and
 glossary. Its Text 23 reprints or re-edits Montgomery 38 and provides a useful example of how one object moves
through successive corpus systems (contents preview).
•  William S. McCullough, Jewish and Mandaean Incantation Bowls in the Royal Ontario Museum (1967). This
 remains a major collection publication (JSTOR record).
•  Charles D. Isbell, Corpus of the Aramaic Incantation Bowls (1975). Useful as a concording compilation of
 then-published material, but no longer reliable as a sole text edition because it inherits and sometimes adds earlier
errors.
The period's prevailing questions were philological and comparative: Which dialect is present? Which demonological or
angelological tradition appears? How does a formula compare with the Talmud, Mandaeism, or older Mesopotamian
religion? Social and archaeological questions were usually secondary.

Formulae (1993) set a higher standard for text edition, translation, and comparative analysis. Their work demonstrated
the importance of formula families, manuscript parallels, and the circulation of Jewish magical traditions across object
types.

4.5 Social, literary, and material turns, 2006-2015
The next decade integrated bowls into broader histories of ancient magic and late antique religion. Gideon Bohak's
Ancient Jewish Magic (2008) and Yuval Harari's later Jewish Magic before the Rise of Kabbalah (English 2017) located
bowls within long Jewish magical traditions without treating rabbinic prohibition as an adequate description of lived
practice. Michael G. Morony emphasized Sasanian Iraqi society and the religiously plural environment; his “Magic and
Society in Late Sasanian Iraq” and “Religion and the Aramaic Incantation Bowls” remain basic social-historical
statements (article record).
The bowls also became evidence for literary creativity. Scholars studied historiolae, first-person narratives, aggressive
curses, formula migration, and named rabbinic wonder-workers. Jason Sion Mokhtarian's work compared bowls with
the Babylonian Talmud while resisting a simple derivation in either direction; Rabbis, Sorcerers, Kings, and Priests
(2015) is a major synthesis (Oxford chapter record).

material, biblical quotations, and technical learning show that at least some practitioners inhabited literate environments
adjacent to other Jewish textual cultures. This does not make all bowl scribes rabbis; it makes the corpus
heterogeneous.

explicit classification criteria more urgent.
Gender and household history became central. Client naming, matronymics, reproductive protection, kin relations, and
first-person grammar offer abundant evidence, but the identity of the person speaking in a spell cannot automatically be
equated with the identity of the person who wrote the object. Dorit Kedar's 2019 dissertation, Who Wrote the Incantation
Bowls?, argues for female authors or scribes in a group of cases (repository record); later work by Ortal-Paz Saar and
Avigail Manekin-Bamberger urges stricter separation among author, textual voice, copyist, producer, client, and
beneficiary. Saar's 2024 survey is a particularly clear methodological statement (open-access article).
Research on biblical quotations also matured. Dorota Molin showed that the bowls are the only epigraphic witnesses to
Biblical Hebrew from late antique Babylonia and can preserve reading traditions, oral transmission, and Aramaic
interference (open-access chapter). Waller's The Bible in the Bowls (2022) catalogued every biblical quotation he could
identify in published JBA bowls as of November 2022, turning a recurrent impression into an auditable dataset
(open-access book).
Finally, provenance could no longer be treated as an external concern. The post-1990 market supplied large
undocumented collections while looting removed find context from Iraq. The Schøyen controversy made the stakes
unusually visible. Beginning in 1995-1996, University College London held 654 bowls from Martin Schøyen's private
collection for research. Media and scholarly investigations questioned whether bowls of probable Iraqi origin had left
Iraq unlawfully after the 1990 invasion of Kuwait; UCL convened a provenance inquiry in 2004. When the bowls were
not promptly returned, the collection sued UCL in 2007. An out-of-court settlement returned the objects and UCL stated
that no adverse claim to title had been made, but the inquiry report was not published. Neil Brodie reports that leaked
findings considered post-1990 removal from Iraq probable; the Schøyen Collection rejects that account and maintains
that the bowls came from a long-held Jordanian collection and were exported from Jordan under a 1988 license. The
unresolved public record is the controversy: legal title, archaeological origin, export history, and scholarly ethics were
repeatedly treated as though one answer settled all four (UCL joint statement; Brodie's documented reconstruction;

4.7 Consolidation and new directions, 2023-2026
The 2025 edited volume Aramaic Incantation Bowls in Their Late Antique Jewish Contexts, edited by Alexander W.
Marcus and Jason Sion Mokhtarian, is the best current gateway to the JBA field. Its chapters cover the publication
history, lexicon, matronymics, gender, motherhood, bodies, families, names, textual variation, liturgy, digital ontology,
and Sasanian Jewish history. Waller's opening “State of the Art” includes a map and publication list through 2024 (table
of contents).
The volume captures several current tendencies:
 •  lexical revision: Ford identifies new lexemes, new meanings, and “ghost words” created by old misreadings;
 •  variation as evidence: Marcus treats divergences as potentially meaningful transmission, not merely scribal
 incompetence;
•  families and naming: Mokhtarian, Ilan, and Burberry use named people and relationships as social evidence;
 •  ritual embodiment: Saar, Eliassian, and Kiperwasser examine gender, motherhood, and bodies without assuming
 that prescriptive categories map directly onto clients;
•  structured data: Walker argues for an ontology capable of relating texts, hands, people, formulae, images, objects,
 collections, and editions;
•  historical anchoring: Bhayro brings the bowls into Sasanian Jewish history and emphasizes absolutely dated
 specimens.
Related work has sharpened the bowl-Talmud comparison. Simcha Gross and Avigail Manekin-Bamberger argue that
Babylonian Jewish society in the bowls is internally diverse and that some scribes were close to learned legal and
literary milieus, making “popular versus rabbinic” an inadequate binary (JQR10 article record). Shai Secunda's 2025
study treats the Talmud and bowls as participants in a wider Babylonian textual culture, emphasizing reworking and
circulation rather than hunting only for exact quotation (article record).
The newest object publications reinforce hybridity and technical re-examination. Gross and Scarlassara's Syriac B9002,
Abudraham's 2026 re-editions, and Rivett and Wilkinson's 2026 study of YHBYH and the Tetragrammaton all revisit
inherited readings and presumed communal boundaries. The last study argues that YHBYH should be treated as an
independent divine name in at least some bowl contexts, rather than automatically as a vocalized form of YHWH (article
DOI). These studies show that the field is still generating basic philological discoveries, not merely applying new
theories to a settled corpus.

5.4 Pahlavi, pseudo-script, and images
These materials remain less comprehensively synthesized. Pahlavi bowls connect the corpus to Iranian language and
religious history. Pseudo-script bowls show that the authority of writing could be visual even where no ordinary text can
be read. Waller's “Curious Characters, Invented Scripts, and … Charlatans?” rejects a blanket assumption that such
bowls are simply incompetent frauds; their signs imitate, exaggerate, or strategically transform script (Journal of Near
Eastern Studies 78 [2019]: 119-139). His related open-access article argues that bowl efficacy often inheres in
writtenness itself (Cambridge Core).
Naama Vilozny's work on drawings - especially demons, bound figures, Lilith, and Ashmedai - has established images
as operative components rather than decoration. Future cataloguing should therefore describe image placement,
orientation, gestures, bindings, labels, and relation to text.
6. Interpretive synthesis: what the evidence
presently supports
The chronological history above shows why the field's questions cannot be separated into isolated
topics. Changes in editing altered what counted as a word; archaeology altered what counted as
context; gender history altered who counted as an agent; and provenance research altered which
historical claims an object can bear. Six connected propositions summarize the strongest present
interpretations.
6.1 Ritual problems were translated into authoritative
actions
Protection is the largest category, but it is not one simple function. Bowls address named demons and liliths, illness and
bodily pain, sleep disturbance, pregnancy and infant survival, marriage and household conflict, property and livelihood,
curses and witchcraft, legal success, separation, subordination, and retaliatory harm. One object may protect a person,
spouse, children, house, animals, and possessions at once. "Apotropaic" and "aggressive" are not opposites when
protection works by returning a curse or coercing its sender.
The recurring verbs reveal a ritual grammar. Harmful beings are bound, sealed, banned, excommunicated, adjured, or
divorced. Avigail Manekin-Bamberger traced verbal parallels among bowl formulae, the Bavli, and surviving legal
documents, showing that divorce, oath, vow, and excommunication language could turn socially recognized acts of
separation into supernatural operations (article record). Her contribution is methodological as much as lexical: legal
speech could circulate beyond formal institutions, so resemblance does not require a rabbinic court to have produced
the object.

6.2 Authority was assembled from names, stories, scripture,
and material form
Bowls invoke the God of Israel; Jesus or Christian powers in some Syriac texts; angels, demons, planets, and Iranian
beings; biblical ancestors; rabbis; and legendary ritual experts. Practitioners combine and transform names because
efficacy could be claimed through identity, sound, spelling, visual pattern, narrative precedent, or accumulation. Moses
is important as lawgiver and master of authorized divine speech. Siam Bhayro's study of Moses in the bowls shows how
practitioners recruit Mosaic legacy without implying that the corpus as a whole claims Mosaic authorship (open PDF).
The distinction between YHWH and YHBYH illustrates why such names must not be normalized too quickly. YHWH is
the four-consonant Tetragrammaton, the biblical name conventionally avoided in Jewish pronunciation. YHBYH is a
different five-consonant written sequence: yod-he-bet-yod-he. Montgomery connected it with Greek spellings such as
Iabē and proposed that it encoded a pronunciation of YHWH; Naveh and Shaked judged that suggestion attractive but
unproved and treated it as another magical divine name. Pilar B. Rivett and Robert J. Wilkinson's 2026 survey widens
the evidence base and shows contexts in which YHBYH behaves independently rather than as a mere spelling of
YHWH (article DOI). Their argument does not establish one historical pronunciation of the Tetragrammaton. It
establishes a safer editorial rule: transcribe YHBYH as written, do not silently replace or translate it as YHWH, and
decide from each syntax and parallel whether the scribe treated it as an independent theonym.
Biblical and liturgical passages contribute another kind of authority, while the vessel, spiral, image, and deposition make
that authority materially durable. Waller's biblical catalogue, Molin's work on reading traditions, Levene's work on liturgy,
and Vilozny's analysis of images collectively show that no single layer - word, voice, picture, or object - should be
assumed to carry the whole ritual act.
6.3 Production ranged from copying to recomposition, and
from speech to visible writing
Repeated hands, formula clusters, shared errors, and near-identical layouts support practitioners, exemplars,
workshops, or local networks. Yet "school" requires several converging features: a formula can travel between hands,
and one hand can use several repertoires. Alexander Marcus treats variation as evidence for transmission and
purposeful adaptation, while James Nathan Ford's lexical corrections show how supposed scribal oddities can instead
be dialect, phonetic spelling, graphic convention, or an editor's ghost word.
Charles Häberl emphasized oral performance and the possibility that some performative utterances record spoken acts.
Waller shifted attention to the way spiral layout, pseudo-script, drawing, durable ink, and deposition make writing
performative in itself (open-access article). These positions define a continuum rather than a forced choice:
remembered formula to inscription; written exemplar to copied bowl; adapted template to personalized object; spoken
act joined to writing; or visually authoritative signs with little ordinary lexical content.

6.4 Households join gender, kinship, health, and economy
Named clients and kin make the corpus unusually rich for microhistory. The bowls record matronymics, marriages,
children, multi-generational households, adversaries, houses, animals, goods, and livelihoods. They do not observe
modern boundaries among medical, psychological, legal, social, and demonic causation: pain, nightmares, reproductive
danger, paralysis, fever, conflict, and supernatural attack can occupy one ritual frame.
Women occur frequently as clients and beneficiaries, especially in material concerning marriage, fertility, pregnancy,
childbirth, infants, and household conflict. Dorit Kedar argues that a set of first-person feminine texts reveals female
authors or scribes. Ortal-Paz Saar's 2024 review and Manekin-Bamberger's 2025 "Magic Formulae and Women's
History" respond by separating author, textual voice, copyist, producer, commissioner, client, and beneficiary, and by
asking what recurrent formulae reveal about gendered fears and agency (article record). This exchange advanced the
field because it converted a binary question - "Were women scribes?" - into roles and claims that can be tested bowl by
bowl.
Prosopography requires the same discipline. One person can occur on several bowls, spelling varies, and catalogue
sigla can disguise duplicates. A social history built only from names will overcount; it needs images, hands, family links,
provenance, and separate uncertainty values.
6.5 The bowls and the Bavli overlap without becoming the
same archive
Bavli is the conventional short name for the Babylonian Talmud, a multi-generational rabbinic compilation of legal
discussion, interpretation, and narrative shaped in late antique Babylonia and transmitted through later redaction. It is
niche terminology because specialists often use it as though it named a single contemporary book. For comparison with
the bowls, three cautions follow: the Bavli is layered rather than a dated snapshot; it speaks through rabbinic literary
conventions rather than household commissions; and a parallel may reflect a shared oral, legal, ritual, or narrative
environment rather than direct copying.
The scholarship moved from an opposition between rabbinic religion and "popular magic," through attempts to identify
bowl scribes as rabbis, to a heterogeneous overlap model. Simcha Gross and Avigail Manekin-Bamberger identify
some bowl scribes with unusual proximity to learned legal and rabbinic milieus. Shai Secunda emphasizes circulation
and reworking across Babylonian textual culture. Jason Sion Mokhtarian compares demons, kings, priests, rabbis, and
stories without collapsing genre. Together their work supports uneven contact: some practitioners display specialist
learning, others do not; a title such as "rabbi" can belong to a client; and exact dependence remains possible only
where wording and structure warrant it.
6.6 Religious plurality and provenance are interpretive
variables
Shared names, formulae, stories, and graphic devices show contact among Jewish, Mandaean, Christian, Iranian, and
other traditions. "Syncretism" is too blunt if it replaces the questions that matter: Was a passage translated across
scripts? Did one hand serve clients from more than one group? Was a powerful name borrowed without its theology?
Did a formula belong to a regional professional repertoire? Recent parallel texts make these questions testable.

Unprovenanced bowls may preserve ancient texts, but they cannot support household or depositional history with the

8. Research gaps with the highest payoff
1.  Publication of excavated museum reserves. Penn's largely unpublished Nippur holdings could radically improve
 typology, household context, and sampling bias.
2.  Mandaic and Syriac parity. JBA now has stronger corpora and thematic tools; comparative conclusions require
 similar object-level infrastructure for Mandaic, Syriac, Pahlavi, and pseudo-script bowls.
3.  Stable identifiers and concordances. One object may have excavation, museum, publication, and dealer numbers.
 A crosswalk is foundational.
4.  Palaeographic chronology. Handwriting groups need anchoring to dated or excavated specimens, with transparent
 image criteria.
5.  Material science. Petrography, clay sourcing, ink chemistry, reflectance transformation imaging, and multispectral
 imaging can distinguish workshops, recover readings, and test authenticity.
6.  Household archaeology. Depositional position, associated architecture, paired bowls, and reuse need spatial
 publication.
7.  Formula genealogy. Digital alignment can show whether shared passages reflect exemplars, memory, local
 workshop practice, or cross-language translation.
8.  Gender across language groups. Most synthesis centers JBA; Mandaic and Syriac evidence needs equal analysis
 and stricter role annotation.
9.  Economic history of practice. Prices, access, professionalization, repeat clients, and workshop organization remain
 mostly inferential.
10.  Ethical publication standards. The field needs consistent provenance statements, image histories, and treatment
 of post-conflict acquisitions.
11.  Digital preservation. Projects need downloadable, versioned data with persistent identifiers so that a website's
 disappearance does not erase scholarly infrastructure.

Conclusion
Incantation bowls are best understood neither as a fossilized folk custom nor as illustrations of one
religious tradition. They are a large, internally varied archive of domestic ritual writing produced in
late antique Mesopotamia. Their texts combine local need with portable professional repertoires; their
scripts and divine names cross boundaries more readily than older communal typologies allowed; and
their physical placement makes the household an essential unit of interpretation.
The field's future lies in joining four forms of evidence that have too often been separated: exact philology, high-quality
images and material study, archaeological/provenance history, and structured comparison across objects. The same
rule should govern every modern adaptation derived from a bowl: identify the object, verify the image and latest edition,
separate ancient text from modern intervention, and preserve the editorial trail.
